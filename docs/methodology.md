Multi-agent systems built from large language models are now assembled wherever a task is too large for one model to hold at once: a planner delegates to workers, a supervisor aggregates their replies, and the agents coordinate by exchanging natural-language messages. The promise is division of labour, but the empirical record is sobering. A grounded-theory analysis of more than 1,600 execution traces across seven frameworks reports task-failure rates between 41% and 86.7%, with inter-annotator agreement of kappa = 0.88, and concludes that the failures are systemic rather than a deficit of the underlying model (Cemri et al., 2025). The finding behind that number is uncomfortable for the field: a single model that can hold an entire task in one context window will usually do better than a team of models forced to coordinate through language, and recent decision-theoretic work proves the point formally, showing that any delegated network of agents sharing the same evidence is dominated by a centralised decision maker with the same information (Cemri et al., 2025; Ao et al., 2026). The only firm justification for splitting a task is therefore that it does not fit. Splitting is not free; it imposes a coordination cost paid at the boundary between agents, where one agent compresses what it knows into a message and another acts on it, and this is where coordination quietly fails. The same structure appears outside artificial systems: when human groups solving a cooperative transport puzzle have their communication restricted, they perform worse than a single person and fall back on greedy short-term moves (Dreyer et al., 2025). The boundary, not the reasoning of any one agent, is the object of interest, and it is exactly the object the field cannot yet measure. The literature can instrument task outcomes, attribute a failure to a step after the event, and detect that a collective shows structure beyond its individual agents, but it has no deployable score for the question that matters at the moment of a handoff: how much usable information does this message carry about what the receiver is about to do? This dissertation measures that quantity. It treats political economy and multi-agent coordination as one problem, how agents with partial, asymmetric information produce a collective outcome, borrows the economic vocabulary of delegation to frame it, and supplies the missing measurement: conditional usable information at a single inter-agent text boundary, scored against an objective, physics-defined outcome.

**Research questions and hypotheses.** The dissertation is organised around four questions, stated here once and developed in the experimental design (Section 9). RQ1, the information gradient: as the communication channel between two agents is degraded, do collective success and step efficiency fall, and does the per-handoff conditional usable information fall with them? The hypotheses are that success and efficiency degrade as bandwidth is cut (H1), and that conditional usable information tracks that degradation and explains variance in the outcome beyond the shared state (H2). RQ2, the measurement primitive: does a boundary score computed after the outcome agree with a prospective version computed from the message and the shared state before the outcome is known (H3), and does a cheap, target-free statistic computable at the moment of the handoff track the offline score closely enough to act on (H4)? RQ3a, external validity: on real multi-agent failure logs, does the boundary score localise the step responsible for a failure better than the weak published attribution baselines (H5)? RQ3b, causal validity: is the relationship between low usable information and failure causal rather than correlational, such that blocking and rewriting low-information handoffs before the receiver acts improves outcomes over a control that blocks the same number of randomly chosen handoffs and a control that fires at random (H6)? The review that follows is organised by problem rather than by chronology, and each strand is read for what it leaves open, so that the questions above arrive as the unfilled position in a mapped field rather than as assertions.

## 1. Measuring what a message carries

A measurement is only as good as its object, and the first problem in studying inter-agent communication is deciding what a useful message is. The metrics most readily to hand are the wrong ones: token overlap and embedding similarity measure whether two pieces of text resemble each other, not whether a message helps a receiver act, and a message can be semantically rich yet operationally empty. The information-theoretic ideal is sharper. The information bottleneck (Tishby, Pereira and Bialek, 1999; Tishby and Zaslavsky, 2015) defines the smallest representation of a source that preserves its predictive content about a target, which is exactly the notion of a minimal sufficient message. The difficulty is that the bottleneck is stated in mutual informations, and mutual information is not reliably estimable at the dimensionality of modern sentence embeddings, which routinely exceed 384 dimensions, with nearest-neighbour estimators degrading beyond roughly ten dimensions (Xu et al., 2020). The bottleneck therefore enters this work as a conceptual frame for what an ideal handoff would contain, not as a quantity anyone computes; treating it otherwise would import an estimator that the dimensionality of the payloads is known to break.

The advance that makes the problem tractable is to make the measure model-relative. V-information (Xu et al., 2020) replaces Shannon's assumption of an unbounded decoder with a specified predictive family, and asks how much an input helps a model in that family predict a target; unlike mutual information it is zero when the family cannot extract the signal, and it is estimable by fitting models, with PAC-style sample guarantees that hold in high dimensions. This is the right honesty for language agents, because a message contains usable information only if the receiving model can read it. Ethayarajh, Choi and Swayamdipta (2022) turn the population-level quantity into an instance-level one, pointwise V-usable information, a per-example score computed as the difference between a label-only and an input-conditioned negative log-likelihood under fitted models. Evaluated on SNLI, MultiNLI and CoLA, the metric tracked task difficulty and surfaced mislabelled and structurally flawed instances, which shows it is sensitive to real structure rather than to noise; the per-instance form is what allows a distribution of good and bad handoffs to be compared rather than a single average. The methodological cost is that the number is relative to the chosen model family and to the encoder, a dependence this dissertation addresses directly rather than hides (Section 8).

One further step is needed for a coordination setting, and it is the step the rest of this work turns on. When two agents share an observation, a board, a codebase, or a physical scene, a probe credited with reading a message may simply be reading state that was already visible to both. Conditional probing (Hewitt et al., 2021) extends V-information to condition on a baseline representation, measuring only the usable information a representation adds over what the baseline already provides; applied to syntax, it isolated what a contextual layer encodes beyond non-contextual word identity. Conditioned on the shared state, the same construction measures what a message adds beyond the board both agents can already see, which is the difference between a handoff that communicates and one that merely echoes. A fine-tuning-free variant estimates the same quantity in-context from a frozen model prompted with exemplars, with estimates stable across exemplar choice (Lu et al., 2023), which matters when probes must be re-estimated often. The decisive observation for this review is that the entire lineage, from bottleneck through V-information and pointwise information to conditional probing, has been applied to dataset difficulty, to probing network layers, and to exemplar selection, but never to a message passed between two agents and never conditioned on a shared coordination state. The construct exists; the place this dissertation puts it does not.

## 2. The coordination tax

If splitting a task is only justified when the task does not fit, the natural question is what splitting costs, and the failure literature now answers it in detail. The Multi-Agent System Failure Taxonomy (Cemri et al., 2025) classifies fourteen failure modes into three families, specification and system-design faults, inter-agent misalignment, and task-verification failures, built from grounded-theory coding of execution traces with kappa = 0.88 and released as MAST-Data, over 1,600 annotated traces across seven frameworks with a validated automatic annotator. Inter-agent misalignment, the family that lives at the boundary, accounts for roughly a third of observed failures, and the authors are explicit that stronger base models will not close the taxonomy because the failures are structural. The result reframes the boundary from an implementation detail into the principal site of failure, and it supplies the substrate this dissertation tests against, but as a diagnosis it stops one step short of the quantity needed: it labels a trace as having failed and assigns a failure family, without scoring how much any single handoff actually communicated.

Attribution at finer grain proves to be hard in a way that is itself informative. The Who&When benchmark (Zhang et al., 2025a) collects failure logs from 127 multi-agent systems, each annotated with the agent responsible for the failure and the decisive error step, defined as the earliest mistake whose correction would have changed failure into success, and evaluates three attribution strategies, an all-at-once pass over the whole log, a binary search, and a step-by-step scan. The best method reaches only 53.5% accuracy in naming the responsible agent and 14.2% in pinpointing the failure step, with some methods below chance, and even strong reasoning models do not reach practical usability. Those two figures are, however, the state of the art as of early 2025 and have since been beaten, so they are used here as a dated floor rather than as live headroom. AgenTracer (Zhang et al., 2025b) trains an 8-billion-parameter tracer with multi-granular reinforcement learning and reports roughly an 18% relative margin over frontier reasoning models on Who&When; TraceElephant's own static and dynamic agentic methods reach 66.7% agent and 33.3% step accuracy when the full trace is available, and 60.6% / 27.6% without ground truth (Chen et al., 2026). The honest general statement, which does survive, is that generic frontier reasoning models remain below roughly 10% on step attribution, so the task is genuinely hard for anything not purpose-built for it. Three things follow. Methodologically, attribution in all of this work is attempted after the outcome is known and from the completed transcript, which is the setting in which a content-based boundary score has the most to add. Substantively, the field can now say where multi-agent systems break, the boundary, and can increasingly say which step broke, but it does so with specialised, trained, trace-consuming machinery, and it still cannot say how much a given handoff communicated as it happened. Comparatively, this means the contribution of a boundary measure is not that it outperforms a purpose-trained tracer at localisation — it very likely will not, and that is not what a measure is for — but that it is a cheap, per-handoff, prospective quantity available at the moment the message is sent, and §7 states the comparison axis that follows from this.

The human-side evidence sharpens the motivation rather than supplying a method. Dreyer et al. (2025) built a real cooperative transport puzzle, a T-shaped load manoeuvred through a three-chamber arena joined by two narrow slits, and compared ant colonies with human groups across sizes. Ants improve with group size; humans do not, and when human communication is restricted to resemble the ants', groups perform worse than a single individual and revert to greedy short-term moves. The restricted-communication result is the substantive hook for this work: degrading the channel did not merely slow the group, it changed its strategy for the worse, which is collective cognition governed by information structure and observed empirically rather than asserted. This dissertation transposes that exact task into a two-agent language-model setting, and it adds the measurement the original could not take, scoring the information present at the channel itself rather than inferring it from the outcome.

## 3. Communication that is measured versus communication that is used

The multi-agent reinforcement learning literature is the source of the methodological discipline this dissertation imports, and its central warning is that communication is routinely over-read. Lowe et al. (2019) showed that standard metrics, the correlation between an agent's messages and its later observations or actions, can register active communication even when the messages have no causal effect on the receiver; agents trained on matrix games produced channels that looked informative by mutual-information measures, yet scrambling the messages left the listener's actions unchanged. The conclusion is exact, and it is the reason this dissertation does not stop at correlation: a statistical relationship between a message statistic and an outcome is not evidence that the message caused the outcome, and only manipulating the channel settles it. Eccles et al. (2019) refine the picture by separating two failures that a single correlational number conflates, a sender that fails to encode information (positive signalling) and a receiver that fails to act on it (positive listening), and show that the two need distinct inductive biases. The distinction matters here because it names the two ways a coordination attempt can fail, a channel that carried no usable signal against a channel that carried a signal the partner did not use, and a conditional usable-information measure can tell them apart, which converts a possible null result into a reportable finding.

Where this dissertation parts company with the reinforcement-learning work is the object being handled, and the closest prior method makes the contrast clean. The Informative Multi-Agent Communication framework (Wang et al., 2020) applies the information bottleneck inside the training loop to learn low-entropy, bandwidth-limited messages, compressing a message encoder while preserving task performance. That is design of a message channel, and it presupposes control of the encoder and a gradient through it. The dissertation does the opposite: it scores whether an arbitrary, frozen-model message in natural language carries enough, at inference, model-relatively, with no access to or training of the sender. The economic counterpart of the same idea appears in information design (Lin et al., 2023), which formulates a signalling game in which one agent influences others by supplying information rather than by changing payoffs, and derives an obedience constraint, a signal matters only if it is both informative and one the receiver is willing to act on. The obedience constraint is the formal statement of an unused signal and maps onto the listening half of the absent-versus-unused distinction. The transfer caveat bounds what these results license: the reinforcement-learning and information-design settings learn continuous or symbol-vector messages optimised end to end, whereas the object of this dissertation is unconstrained natural-language text from a pre-trained model, so the literature supplies the questions, the causal discipline, and the absent-versus-unused vocabulary, but not a measurement method that transfers wholesale.

## 4. Language models as spatial coordinators

Transposing a physical coordination task to language models is only sensible if such models can reason about space at all, and the evidence is that they can, within bounds the design must respect. RoCo (Mandi et al., 2023) gives each robot in a multi-arm team a language model that discusses strategy and proposes waypoints, while a separate planner executes low-level motion and feeds back collision checks for in-context replanning; a controlled experiment shows a strong model planning collision-free multi-agent paths in a 3D grid, with performance falling as grid size and obstacle count grow. The lesson is a workable division of labour, the language model negotiates strategy and a non-language component handles precise geometry, and a warning that performance is sensitive to how much geometric load is placed on the model. This dissertation keeps the negotiation but, unlike RoCo, has the agents choose discrete actions directly rather than delegating motion to a planner, which keeps the message causally coupled to the outcome being scored, and it treats the way the scene is written into the prompt as an experimental factor rather than a fixed choice, because that formatting is one of the things RoCo shows will move the result.

The limits are as instructive as the capability. The LLM-Coordination benchmark (Agashe et al., 2024) decomposes coordination into separable sub-skills, environment comprehension, theory-of-mind reasoning about a partner's beliefs and intentions, and joint planning, and shows that current models are uneven across them: they do well where decisions rest on explicit, observable variables and degrade sharply where success requires inferring a partner's unstated intentions, a measured theory-of-mind gap. The same work finds that language agents generalise to unseen partners more readily than reinforcement-learning agents that overfit their training partners, so the deficit is specific rather than total. The consequence for this dissertation is a groundedness check, a test that messages reflect the true state rather than hallucinated geometry, since a usable-information score computed over ungrounded messages would be measuring noise. Two further works fix the contrast with what is measured here. CoELA (Zhang et al., 2024) builds a modular embodied agent whose components communicate in natural language and improves cooperation in simulated homes, but there language is the mechanism of coordination, not the object of measurement; the framework instruments outcomes, never the information content of the handoff. ConPoSe (Steinkrüger et al., 2025) is the closest physical neighbour, using a language model's common-sense reasoning with local search to select robot-object contact points for cooperative pushing of cuboids, cylinders, and, tellingly, T-shapes, with up to fifteen robots and better scaling than the analytical baseline. The contrast is exact: ConPoSe has a single guiding reasoner over a search procedure and no inter-agent negotiation in language, so the message between two agents and its usable information about the next outcome, the object of this dissertation, does not exist in it.

## 5. Measuring collectives versus measuring boundaries

The most active frontier in 2025 and 2026 studies whole populations of language-model agents, and it is also where the closest measurement neighbour sits, which makes stating the difference precisely most of the positioning argument. Riedl (2025) introduces a data-driven information-theoretic test for whether a collective is more than the sum of its agents, operationalised as a partial information decomposition of time-delayed mutual information that separates redundant, unique, and synergistic contributions, applied to a guessing game with minimal feedback and no direct communication. The result is twofold and careful: cross-agent synergy can be detected information-theoretically, and the control condition showed temporal coupling without coordinated alignment, that is, structure without coordination, which guards against reading correlation as teamwork. The contrast with this dissertation is the sharpest in the review. Riedl measures system-level synergy across a whole collective, with no single boundary and no communication channel to score; this dissertation measures decision-relevant usable information at one inter-agent text boundary against an objective outcome. The two are different objects, and the partial-information-decomposition machinery, which Williams and Beer (2010) defined to split multivariate information into redundant, unique, and synergistic atoms, is the right tool for Riedl's question and the wrong granularity for this one; it is named here to fix that distinction, not used as a metric. Riedl also confines mutual-information decomposition to low-dimensional behavioural variables, which agrees with this dissertation's decision to keep mutual information and transfer entropy for low-dimensional action trajectories and never for text payloads.

At the population scale, coordination is often operationalised as agreement rather than as task success, and the convention-emergence literature shows both the power and the limit of that move. Ashery et al. (2025) place decentralised populations of language-model agents in a naming game with a coordination incentive and find that they spontaneously converge on shared conventions through pairwise interaction, that collective biases can emerge even when individuals are unbiased, and that a committed minority can tip an established convention past a threshold, all through pairwise natural-language exchange rather than any central controller. This is rigorous population-scale science, and it marks the end of the spectrum this dissertation does not occupy, no spatial task, no physics outcome, and coordination measured as symbol agreement rather than as usable information about an outcome. Taken together, Riedl and Ashery establish that the frontier is busy at the level of the collective and the convention, and that the dyadic, physics-grounded, boundary-level position taken here is distinct and, as of this review, unfilled. The bridge to the economics of the next section is visible in both: local interactions under partial information aggregating into a collective outcome is the political-economy question, posed in a multi-agent register.

## 6. Information asymmetry and the economics of delegation

The deepest framing of the problem is economic, and it supplies both a vocabulary for what goes wrong at a boundary and a decision-theoretic statement of what the right measurement is. Rauba et al. (2026) argue that a language-model multi-agent system is a principal-agent problem: a supervising principal delegates subtasks to specialised agents and aggregates their replies, and the defining property is information asymmetry, because agents hold task-specific information and reasoning traces the principal cannot see through a finite context window. The failure quantity is agency loss, the gap between the principal's intended outcome and the system's realised behaviour, and the paper maps the machine-learning vocabulary of scheming and covert subversion onto the economic concepts of moral hazard and adverse selection. The argument carries an explicit assumption, that asymmetry is harmless when incentives are aligned because aligned agents report truthfully, and it is precisely this assumption that this dissertation treats as an empirical claim rather than a theorem. Truthful reporting through a narrow channel can still fail to carry the information the principal's outcome depends on, which is agency loss without misalignment, and conditional usable information is the instrument that measures that residual. The half of the principal-agent picture concerned with deception and divergent goals is named here as out of scope and as future work; the aligned-incentive half is the uninstrumented case this work fills.

The decision-theoretic backing turns this from a framing into a justification for a specific measure. A recent analysis of the reliability limits of delegated multi-agent planning proves that, under any proper scoring rule, the gap between the value a centralised decision maker achieves and the value achievable after communication is exactly an expected posterior distortion, the divergence between the posterior given full information and the posterior given the communicated information, which under logarithmic loss reduces to a conditional mutual information (Ao et al., 2026). Two consequences bear on this dissertation. First, the paper shows that coarse proxies for communication, transcript length, number of rounds, number of roles, are not the relevant object for reliability, and that two systems with the same number of stages can have very different communication loss depending on whether they preserve the decision-relevant posterior; this is an independent argument that a content measure at the boundary, not a structural count, is what reliability requires. Second, it identifies the ideal quantity, the conditional information a message carries about the outcome, as the same object the measurement lineage of Section 1 approximates in an estimable, model-relative form. The economics therefore does not only motivate the work; it names the target, and the next section shows that conditional usable information is the defensible way to estimate that target at the dimensionality of language.

## 7. The research gap

Read across its six strands, the field has assembled every component of the question this dissertation asks and has never put them together. The measurement lineage gives a tractable, model-relative, instance-level, baseline-conditioned score, but applies it to dataset difficulty and probing, never to a message between agents. The reinforcement-learning literature supplies the discipline that communication must be demonstrated causally and the distinction between an absent and an unused signal, but its methods learn messages rather than score arbitrary ones. The embodied-coordination work shows that language models can negotiate spatial tasks within bounds and that the closest physical neighbour, contact-point selection on T-shaped loads, has no negotiated boundary to measure. The frontier measures synergy across whole collectives and conventions across whole populations, never decision-relevant information at one boundary against an outcome. The failure literature establishes that the boundary is the principal site of breakdown and that attributing a failure to a step is hard even for purpose-trained models, but every method in it consumes a completed trace and none supplies a per-handoff score of what the message carried. The economics frames the problem as delegation under information asymmetry and proves that the right object is the posterior distortion a message induces, yet leaves the aligned-incentive case uninstrumented. The unfilled position is therefore specific and small: conditional usable information, scored and then enforced at a single inter-agent natural-language boundary, against an objective outcome.

This dissertation occupies that position through three studies, each answering one of the questions flagged above. The first measures whether degrading the channel between two agents lowers both task success and the conditional usable information at the handoff, which establishes that the construct moves with the thing it should move with. The second establishes the measure as a primitive, testing whether a score computed after the outcome agrees with one computable before it, and whether a cheap statistic available at the moment of the handoff tracks the offline ground truth well enough to act on. The third tests external and causal validity, asking whether the same boundary score localises the responsible step in real failure logs better than the weak published baselines, and whether blocking low-information handoffs at runtime improves outcomes against matched controls, which is the intervention the causal critique demands. The contribution is sized deliberately: not a new information measure, but the first application of conditional usable information to a coordination handoff, conditioned on the shared state so that the score reflects what a message adds rather than what it echoes, together with the demonstration that the same quantity can be estimated cheaply enough to act on at runtime.

One adjacency must be stated precisely rather than left for a reviewer to raise, because it is the nearest thing in the literature to the prospective half of this dissertation's claim. Failure attribution has begun to move from post-hoc analysis to online auditing: AgentForesight (Zhang et al., 2026) reframes attribution so that an auditor sees only the prefix of an unfolding trajectory at each step and must either continue the run or alarm at the earliest decisive error, explicitly to recover the intervention window that post-hoc attribution forfeits. That work and this dissertation therefore share the pre-outcome setting, and any claim resting on prospectivity alone would now be contested. Three differences survive the overlap and together define the position taken here. First, object: AgentForesight audits a trajectory prefix and predicts a step and an agent; this dissertation scores a single message at a single boundary and reports how many bits it added, which is a measure with units rather than a detector with a verdict. Second, construction: AgentForesight is a 7-billion-parameter model trained with reinforcement learning on a purpose-built corpus, whereas the quantity here is a log-loss difference between two small probes on frozen embeddings, and its cheapest runtime proxy is a cosine — a difference of several orders of magnitude in cost that is itself the operating characteristic worth reporting. Third, and decisively, intervention: an alarm halts a run and reports a prediction, but it does not block or rewrite the message before the receiver acts, and it is not validated against a matched-firing-rate control. The causal arm of this dissertation does exactly that, which is the standard Lowe et al. (2019) set and which no online-auditing result has yet met. The comparison to make, then, is on the operating characteristic — what localisation is obtained per unit of compute, whether any of it is available before the outcome exists, and whether acting on it changes the outcome — and not on raw localisation accuracy against a specialised tracer. That framing is both more defensible and more informative, and it is the axis on which Sections 9.8 and 9.9 report.

The sections that follow give the methodology for the measure and the design of the studies that exercise it.

## 8. Methodology: why conditional usable information is a defensible measure

### 8.1 The target the decision theory names, and why it is not mutual information

The measure this dissertation uses is conditional pointwise V-usable information, and the case for it is that it is the estimable form of the quantity the field's own decision theory says is correct. The argument has four steps. The target is fixed by the reliability-limits result of Section 6: the information lost when a message replaces full observation of the outcome-relevant state is, under a proper scoring rule, an expected posterior distortion, and under logarithmic loss that distortion is a conditional mutual information between the message and the outcome given what is already known (Ao et al., 2026). The quantity to measure is therefore the conditional information a handoff carries about the next outcome given the shared state, not a proxy such as message length or round count, which the same result shows are the wrong object. The obstacle is estimation: this conditional mutual information is defined over sentence embeddings of several hundred dimensions, and at that scale it is not reliably estimable, with nearest-neighbour estimators known to break beyond roughly ten dimensions (Xu et al., 2020). The resolution is to make the measure model-relative rather than absolute. V-information (Xu et al., 2020) asks how much a message helps a specified, bounded predictive family predict the outcome, is zero when that family cannot extract the signal, and is estimable from data by fitting models, with PAC-style sample guarantees that hold in high dimensions; its pointwise form (Ethayarajh et al., 2022) gives a per-handoff score, and its conditional form (Hewitt et al., 2021) subtracts a baseline so that only information beyond the shared state is counted. Conditional pointwise V-usable information is the composition of these three, and it estimates, model-relatively, exactly the conditional-information target the decision theory identifies.

It is worth being explicit about what the model-relativity buys, because it is usually read as a weakness and is in fact the property that makes the measure appropriate here. A Shannon mutual information asks whether the message and the outcome are statistically dependent under an unbounded decoder. That is the wrong question for a coordination boundary: a message that encodes the goal position in a cipher the receiving model cannot read is, to Shannon, maximally informative, and to the receiver, worthless. V-information asks instead how much the message helps a *specified, bounded* family predict the outcome, and returns zero when that family cannot extract the signal. Usable information for an agent is exactly information that agent's model can use, so the model-relative reading is the semantically correct one, not a concession to estimability. The concession that does have to be made is a different one, and it is made openly in §8.8: the number carries the fingerprint of the probe family and the encoder, so both are pinned, both are reported, and sensitivity to the encoder is measured rather than assumed away.

### 8.2 The construct, stated for a handoff

Stated concretely, let *s* be the state, *m* the message under test, and *Y* the outcome of the next joint action. Fit two probes from the chosen family: one, *g*, on the state and the message together, and one, *g′*, on the state alone, each predicting *Y*. The conditional usable information of the message is the per-instance log-loss reduction the message buys,

> CPVI(*m* → *Y* | *s*) = log₂ *g*[*s*, *m*](*Y*) − log₂ *g′*[*s*](*Y*)

and its mean over handoffs is the conditional V-information. Put plainly, train one small model that sees the board and the message and one that sees only the board, and measure how much better the first predicts what happens next; the difference is what the message added beyond the board. Three properties of this definition matter downstream and are stated here so that later sections can rely on them. It is *signed*: a message that misleads the conditional probe scores negative, which is the correct behaviour and which the prospective twin of §9.4 cannot reproduce. It is *pointwise*: the object of study is the distribution of per-handoff scores across a condition, not only its mean, and the tails are where the absent-versus-unused decomposition of §9.6 lives. And it is *paired*: the unconditional score, PVI, which omits the baseline, is computed on the same handoffs, and the gap PVI − CPVI is reported everywhere CPVI is reported. That gap is not bookkeeping. It is the quantitative statement of how much of a message's apparent value was an echo of state the receiver already held, and on a task where both agents see a shared board it is expected to be large. Reporting message value without its state-only baseline is the single easiest way to overstate a communication result, and this dissertation treats the two as one artefact.

### 8.3 Whose state is *s*? The receiver-conditioning decision

The construct above is under-specified in one place that turns out to matter more than any other single choice in the project: when the two agents do not see the same thing, whose state does the baseline probe receive? The decision taken here, and pre-registered before any data was recorded, is that ***s* is the state observable to the receiver**, not to the sender and not to an omniscient observer.

The reasoning is that CPVI is meant to answer a receiver-side question — how much did this message add to what the agent about to act already knew — and any other choice answers a different question. The consequence is sharpest in the asymmetric-visibility condition (C3, §9.2), where the sender sees the whole arena and the receiver sees only a local window. Under receiver conditioning, information the sender holds and the receiver does not is exactly the information the message can add, so C3 is the condition designed to give the message the most to add — an expectation the pilot has already complicated (E3-local measured C3's CPVI below C0's; §9.2) and the bf16 re-gate tests rather than assumes. Under sender conditioning the measured question changes rather than the score merely shrinking: the baseline probe would be handed the goal geometry the C3 message uniquely carries, so the score would answer what the message adds to what the *sender* knew — not a coordination question — and the condition's designed asymmetry would go unmeasured. This is an argument about which question is asked, not a claim that any score is zero by construction: usable information is probe-relative, and the pilot measured +0.19 bits in C0 where the two views coincide.

This is not a hypothetical. The implementation conditioned on the sender's full state until it was corrected in August 2026, and the defect is recorded in `docs/experiment_design_log.md`. It is reported here rather than quietly fixed because it demonstrates the general point that a conditional measure is only as meaningful as the conditioning set, and because it changes how one cross-condition comparison must be read. Under C3 the baseline probe is fitted against a *smaller* observation than under C0–C2 and C4, so C3's CPVI is measured against its own receiver's baseline rather than against a common one. Cross-condition comparisons involving C3 are therefore comparisons of *added* information against each condition's own baseline, not comparisons of absolute magnitude on a shared scale, and the results chapter says so wherever C3 appears. The alternative — holding the baseline fixed across conditions — was considered and rejected, because it would make the baseline probe strictly better informed than the agent whose behaviour is being predicted, which is precisely the incoherence the receiver-conditioning rule exists to prevent.

### 8.4 Estimation: cross-fitting, and the leakage a naive split would create

Per-handoff scores require each handoff to be scored by a probe that did not see it, so the estimator cross-fits: the corpus is partitioned into folds, both probes are fitted on the complement of each fold, and every handoff receives its score from the model that held it out. The partition is *grouped*, with the group key being the episode identifier, and this is load-bearing rather than fastidious. Handoffs from one episode share a trajectory: consecutive states are near-duplicates, the same goal geometry recurs, and the outcome labels are autocorrelated along the path. A random handoff-level split would put step 4 of an episode in training and step 5 in test, and the conditional probe would be rewarded for memorising the trajectory rather than for reading the message. The inflation this produces is not small and it flatters exactly the quantity the thesis reports, so grouped splitting is the default and is not configurable away. On real logs (§9.8) the same discipline applies with the trace identifier as the group key; the leakage argument is identical and only the name of the group changes.

Two further estimator choices follow from the same concern. Stratification within the grouped split keeps the outcome base rate roughly constant across folds, without which small folds can be nearly single-class and the log-loss difference becomes unstable. And per-handoff scores are averaged over *R* repeated cross-fits with different fold seeds, with the across-repeat standard deviation retained per handoff as that score's measurement error. A single cross-fit gives each handoff its score from exactly one fitted model, and at pilot sample sizes the sign of an individual score can flip under nothing more than probe reseeding — which matters because those per-handoff scores are the mediator in the H2 analysis and the ground truth in all of RQ2. Logistic refits at this scale cost milliseconds, so the repetition is nearly free; *R* is fixed at pre-registration and the reported score is the repeat mean.

### 8.5 Probe validity: three different nulls, answering three different objections

A probe that predicts well might be reading the representation, or it might simply be a capable model that learned the task from whatever it was given. Distinguishing these is the standard hazard of the probing literature, and this design carries three separate checks, each answering an objection the others do not. Conflating them is common and would leave two of the three objections open.

| Check | Construction | The objection it answers | Pre-registered expectation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shuffled-message null | Permute messages within condition, holding state and label fixed, and re-estimate CPVI | "The message is not doing the work; any text in that slot would score the same" | CPVI collapses to approximately zero |
| Control-task selectivity | Replace *Y* with a random label stratified to the observed base rate, refit both probes, and report the resulting CPVI and the gap selectivity = CPVI − CPVI<sub>control</sub> | "Your probe family can manufacture apparent information from noise at this sample size" | Control CPVI approximately zero; selectivity approximately equal to CPVI |
| Held-out versus training AUROC | Report the conditional probe's training AUROC alongside its held-out AUROC | "The probe is overfitting the concatenated feature vector" | A small gap; a large one triggers the capacity rule below |

The middle row is the addition that the design previously lacked and is the one an examiner from an NLP background will ask for by name. Hewitt and Liang (2019) show that probe accuracy alone cannot separate "the representation encodes this" from "the probe learned the task", and that the remedy is a control task whose labels are random by construction, so that any accuracy above chance is memorisation capacity rather than signal. With 1,536-dimensional concatenated features and a few hundred pilot handoffs, that capacity is a live risk and no amount of held-out AUROC settles it, because a probe can generalise and still be reading its own capacity rather than the message. Selectivity is reported alongside every headline CPVI figure and appears in the figure captions.

The selection rule that follows is fixed in advance rather than chosen on inspection, because "we reduced probe capacity until the control check passed" is a researcher degree of freedom unless the rule is stated first. The pre-registered rule is: if control-task CPVI is materially above zero, increase regularisation by a fixed schedule and, if that does not resolve it, fall back to the lowest-capacity probe in the family; the choice is made on pilot data only, before the freeze, and the rule and its outcome are both reported. This matters because the honest answer to "how did you choose *C*?" has to be something other than "it worked".

### 8.6 Length as a confound, and why C1 dissociates rather than confirms it

"CPVI is a fancy word count" is the first sceptical question this construct will attract, and the design makes the confound structural rather than incidental, because C1 caps message length directly. Leaving that to a rebuttal at viva would be a mistake; it belongs in the pre-registration. Two controls are fixed in advance. First, the partial Spearman correlation of CPVI with the outcome, controlling for message token length, is reported alongside the uncontrolled correlation. Second, message length enters the mediation as a covariate on the path from CPVI to outcome, and both the controlled and uncontrolled indirect effects are reported.

Done this way the confound inverts into evidence. If CPVI were a proxy for length, then within C1 — where length is clamped to a narrow band — CPVI would have little variance and no relationship to the outcome. If instead CPVI continues to discriminate outcomes among messages of near-identical length, the construct and the confound have dissociated, and C1 becomes the cell that demonstrates the measure rather than the cell that threatens it. The emergent-communication literature faces the same problem with channel capacity as a confound and handles it with matched ablations, so the move is well precedented.

### 8.7 What the measure cannot do, and the object that replaces it at runtime

CPVI needs the realised outcome, because *Y* appears in both terms of its definition. It is therefore an offline, audit-grade quantity, and it cannot be thresholded at the moment of a handoff no matter how cheap the probes are. This is a hard property of the construct and not an engineering limitation, and it is the reason the runtime gate of §9.5 thresholds a different quantity entirely.

The prospective counterpart used in RQ2 is likewise a different object, and describing it as "the same probe applied earlier" would be inaccurate. It is the per-instance Kullback–Leibler divergence, in bits, from the conditional probe's predictive distribution to the state-only probe's, capped to keep it finite:

> twin(*s*, *m*) = KL( *g*[*s*, *m*] ‖ *g′*[*s*] )

This is the expected CPVI of the handoff under the conditional probe's own belief about the outcome, taken before the outcome exists. Its signature takes no *Y* at all, which makes the no-outcome discipline structural rather than a convention someone could violate later. Its consequential property is that a KL divergence is non-negative while retrospective CPVI is signed, so the two cannot agree in the region that matters most: a message that confidently misleads produces a large positive twin and a large negative CPVI. This one-sidedness is not a defect to be reported apologetically; it is a finding about the limits of runtime measurement, and it is pre-registered as such. The H3 agreement analysis is therefore stratified by the sign of the retrospective score, and the expected result is strong agreement on the positive stratum and structural disagreement on the negative one — which is precisely the statement that a decision-time measure can tell you a message is confident, and cannot tell you it is wrong.

### 8.8 Honesty constraints, and what is frozen before the main runs

Four constraints are carried openly rather than buried, because each bears on whether the measurement is sound.

The score is **model-relative and encoder-relative** by construction, so it is reported as such and its sensitivity to the encoder is measured, by re-estimating the headline quantities under a second pinned encoder rather than by assertion. The encoder and its revision are pinned to a commit hash and recorded in every run manifest; embeddings are computed once, cached by content hash, and frozen before any probe is fitted, so no probe fit can perturb the representation it is reading.

The measure is **offline by construction**, per §8.7, and the runtime statistic that can be thresholded is a separate, target-free quantity calibrated against realised outcomes. Keeping the two distinct is what stops a runtime decision from depending on information it could not have had.

The **conditioning is on the receiver's state**, per §8.3, which is what makes the construct a statement about coordination rather than a relabelling of dataset difficulty, and the design includes a condition (C3) in which the receiver's observation is genuinely restricted, so that the conditioning set varies across the design rather than being held constant. The stronger claim — that without such a condition the conditional measure would be near zero by construction — is deliberately *not* made, and the pilot is the reason: CPVI is V-*usable* information, defined against a bounded probe family, and a message can add usable information even where the receiver holds the sender's state verbatim. E3-local measures +0.19 bits in exactly that case.

Finally, the **outcome variable *Y* and the probe family *V* are frozen before the main runs**, together with the encoder revision, the serialisation, the channel parameters and the prompt version. Re-selecting *Y* or *V* after seeing the main-run results would be the clearest available form of researcher degrees of freedom, since both are choices that demonstrably move the headline number. The freeze is a dated, committed pre-registration document; the pilot exists precisely so that the choices it fixes are made on pilot data, and any deviation after the freeze is logged with its reason rather than absorbed silently. With the measure, its checks and its limits fixed, the design that exercises it follows.

## 9. Experimental design

The design exercises the measure across four studies in priority order, on a single controlled task whose outcome is defined by physics, with the same handoff serving as the unit of measurement throughout. The components are described first, then each study in turn. Every numeric parameter quoted below is the value implemented in the repository and recorded in the run manifest, not a design aspiration; where the implemented value departs from the design as first specified, §10.5 says so and gives the reason.

### 9.1 Task and environment

The task is a two-agent transposition of the cooperative transport puzzle of Dreyer et al. (2025), implemented as a deterministic two-dimensional rigid-body simulation in Pymunk. The arena is top-down and gravity-free with damping below unity, so the load loses momentum when it is not being pushed and the puzzle is quasi-static, matching the ant-and-human task in which the load does not coast. Three chambers are separated by static walls leaving two narrow slit gaps; a T-shaped load, built as one rigid body carrying a crossbar of 1.4 by 0.3 and a stem of 0.3 by 1.0 so that its mass and moment of inertia are those of a single rigid object, starts in the first chamber and must reach the third. Because the physics is deterministic given a seed, the outcome against which a message is scored is a real event rather than a scripted label.

**Difficulty is threading clearance, and it is not rotation.** The original design specified that the T "must rotate to pass a narrow slit", and that intent is geometrically false — a fact established here by an exhaustive A\* search over the macro-action space on the real simulator, and then confirmed from first principles. To cross a thin wall, the vertical slice of each member of the T at the instant it crosses the wall plane must fit inside the gap. The crossbar and the stem are perpendicular, so whichever member is aligned with the direction of travel crosses at its 0.3 thickness while the other is then perpendicular to travel and presents its full length across the gap. Rotation only swaps which member jams. The tightest slit the T can thread is therefore the length of its *shorter* member, the stem at 1.0, and that threshold is invariant to orientation; conversely a slit at or above the full vertical extent of 1.3 clears head-on with no manoeuvre at all. The difficulty ladder is accordingly defined by clearance above the 1.0 threading threshold: slit widths of **1.8 (easy), 1.2 (medium) and 1.1 (hard)**, so that easy clears head-on and medium and hard both sit in the threading regime, graded by how precisely the manoeuvre must be aimed. This is a cleaner information gradient than the original construction, because difficulty now increases through the precision the coordination must achieve rather than through a manoeuvre the geometry does not require. The point is stated plainly in the results chapter as well, since a reviewer who works the geometry will otherwise ask why rotation was ever the difficulty knob.

**Actions, budgets and the oracle.** Each turn a single discrete macro-action is selected from seven: a step in each of the four cardinal directions, a rotation in either direction, and a wait. Each is realised as a world-frame impulse applied at the centre of mass, after which the space is stepped until it settles under damping. The angular impulse is 0.5, giving approximately 34 degrees per rotation action; at the originally shipped value of 2.0 a single rotation turned the T by roughly 135 degrees against its small moment of inertia, leaving only orientations at multiples of 45 degrees reachable and making the threading manoeuvre unaimable. The same A\* search that established the clearance threshold also certifies the task: it returns oracle-optimal solutions of **7, 13 and 13 macro-actions** for easy, medium and hard, against per-difficulty step budgets of **18, 33 and 33**, set at roughly 2.5 times the optimum. A regression test re-derives these optima on every commit, so a future change to the physics that renders a difficulty infeasible fails loudly rather than silently converting into an uninterpretable null. The discrete interface keeps the negotiation causally coupled to the outcome and makes each handoff a clean unit to score; a continuous force-composition interface was considered and rejected because it introduces force composition as a second confound alongside the channel.

**Replication and the start-pose jitter.** With greedy decoding, deterministic physics and a fixed start pose, two episodes at different seeds issue byte-identical prompts at every step, so a seed axis would be pseudo-replication: the mixed model's seed random effect would have no variance to absorb and the probe would be fitted on near-duplicate rows. The design therefore applies a **seeded start-pose jitter** — initial *x* in [1.2, 2.8], *y* in [1.5, 4.5] and orientation in [−π/2, +π/2] — keyed on the seed alone, so that the same jittered start is paired across conditions and the channel remains the only manipulated variable. This single change supplies genuine replication, diversifies the probe's training data, and improves outcome-class balance for the pilot's signal gate.

**Outcome.** Success is the load reaching the goal chamber within its budget. Progress is the reduction in geodesic distance to the goal, computed over a waypoint graph through the slit centres rather than as Euclidean distance, which would reward pressing the load against a wall. Collision and stall are detected from contact and velocity.

### 9.2 Agents and the degradable channel

Each agent is an instance of an open-weight language model served locally behind a high-throughput inference endpoint, with greedy decoding, a fixed seed and a revision pinned to a commit hash. The primary configuration is self-play, the same model in both roles, which isolates the effect of the channel from any difference between models; one robustness cell uses a heterogeneous pair.

On each turn agent A observes the serialised state and emits a natural-language message. Agent B observes its own (possibly degraded) view of the state together with the delivered message and selects the joint macro-action under guided JSON decoding against a fixed schema, which removes parser brittleness from the action channel entirely. **B decides alone, informed by A's handoff**; there is no acknowledgement or agreement round. This is a deliberate narrowing of the original specification, in which the two agents "must agree" an action: a reply from B would create a second inter-agent boundary, and with two boundaries per turn the unit of measurement stops being well defined. One message, one action, one score.

That single message is the handoff this dissertation scores, and the communication conditions degrade it and nothing else, so any difference in outcome is attributable to the channel rather than to the task or the models. The ladder is implemented as five conditions, of which four are degradations:

| Condition | Degradation | Implemented parameter | Why it is in the design |
|---|---|---|---|
| C0 | None — passthrough | — | The ceiling, and the reference for every contrast |
| C1 | Bandwidth cap | Truncation to 8 whitespace tokens | Bandwidth, and the structural home of the length confound (§8.6) |
| C2 | Latency | Delivery delayed by one step | Staleness: the message describes a state that has moved on |
| C3 | Asymmetric visibility | B's observation windowed to ±2 grid rows; the message is left intact | The floor-effect guard: it forces the message to carry information the receiver's state does not contain |
| C4 | Lexical noise | Seeded token dropout at rate 0.4 | Resilience of usable information to corruption |

Two properties of this table are load-bearing. First, C3 is the only condition that touches B's observation rather than the message, and it is included so that the receiver's observation — the thing CPVI conditions on — varies across the design instead of being a constant. An earlier version of this argument ran that without C3 the conditional score would have nothing to be conditional about; the pilot falsified it, measuring +0.19 bits in C0 where the receiver holds the sender's state verbatim, because a bounded probe cannot extract from a state embedding what a message states outright (D20). Second, C4 is dropout only. The original design offered "token dropout *or* paraphrase corruption", and the paraphrase variant is not implemented, because paraphrasing requires a model call inside the channel, which would make the channel neither cheap nor deterministic and would introduce a third model into a two-agent study.

Beyond these, the channel implementation is constrained never to reach into physics state or the action path. This is enforced by construction rather than by convention, and it is what licenses the causal reading of every C*k* versus C0 contrast.

### 9.3 Outcome variable and probe family

The outcome variable has a **primary and a continuous twin**, so the study is not hostage to a single label choice. Four candidates are computed and persisted for every handoff, and the primary is fixed at pre-registration:

| Label | Definition | Role |
|---|---|---|
| `y_binary_progress` | Did the next *k* = 3 steps make net positive geodesic progress? | **Primary.** Stable, interpretable, yields an AUROC |
| `y_continuous_displacement` | The signed change in geodesic distance over the same window | The unthresholded twin; guards against a saturating binary label |
| `y_discrete_config` | The chamber occupied at the end of the window | Captures coarse configuration structure |
| `y_terminal_success` | Did the episode ultimately succeed from here? | Closest to the true objective, but a long credit path and a weak per-handoff signal |

The horizon *k* is the only free knob in the primary label and is fixed at pre-registration on pilot data. It is set to 3 rather than 1 because a single macro-action under damping frequently produces displacement below the noise floor of the settling dynamics, so *k* = 1 yields a label that is close to a coin flip in exactly the states where coordination matters most; *k* = 3 buys a stable label at the cost of a slightly longer credit path, and the trade is recorded rather than assumed.

The predictive family is kept small and tractable, because the dimensionality argument that rules out mutual-information estimation also demands an estimator that can actually be fitted. The defaults are **L2-regularised logistic regression at *C* = 1.0** for the binary label and a linear regressor for the continuous twin, both on concatenated frozen sentence embeddings of the receiver's observation and the delivered message, with a two-layer network held in reserve if the linear probe underfits. The encoder is **`BAAI/bge-base-en-v1.5` at 768 dimensions**, pinned by revision; a 1024-dimensional encoder was available and was rejected, because at pilot sample sizes the wider feature vector materially raises the probe-capacity risk of §8.5 without a corresponding gain. The continuous probe is homoscedastic rather than heteroscedastic as originally specified; the deviation is recorded in the configuration object and the heteroscedastic path raises an explicit error rather than silently substituting the simpler model, so the deviation is auditable rather than invisible. It will be implemented only if the pilot's continuous twin misbehaves.

**Serialisation is an experimental factor, not a fixed choice.** The same state is written into the prompt in three forms: numeric tuples, an ASCII occupancy grid at a cell size of 0.25 — chosen so that the T's 0.3-thick members remain approximately one cell wide and the threading affordance is visible at all — and a templated natural-language description. The three are *matched in task-relevant information up to representation precision*, not strictly isomorphic: the grid quantises pose to its cell size and expresses orientation only through the rendered footprint. The claim made in the results is the matched one, because the isomorphism claim is false and easily falsified by a reader. A transcript read of the first live episodes found the matching broken in the other direction as well, and more seriously: the numeric form printed the load pose, its velocity and the goal, but named neither the internal walls nor the slit, so the form a reader would assume the most complete was the one arm in which the sender could not describe the obstacle it was negotiating. It now prints the wall abscissae and the slit's *y*-interval, the matched claim holds as stated across all three forms, and the correction is recorded as D19 and in the prompt-surface version. A second omission of the same kind was found in the pilot and closed before the main sweep: the state named the *gap's* extent and never the *load's*, so the band of positions that can actually thread a slit — ±0.25 about the centre for a 1.3-tall load in an 1.8 gap, not the full ±0.9 — was not derivable from the prompt, and a sender was observed calling a load aligned with a slit it could not fit. The numeric form now states the load's dimensions; it does not state the derived threading band, which would perform the agents' inference rather than describe their scene.

### 9.4 The estimator and its prospective twin

Per-handoff CPVI is estimated as defined in §8.2 and cross-fitted as described in §8.4, with the episode identifier as the group key, stratification on the outcome, and averaging over *R* repeated fold seeds. Each handoff receives a signed score in bits; the unconditional PVI and the PVI − CPVI gap are computed on the same handoffs and reported alongside. The classification form additionally reports the held-out AUROC of the conditional probe against the state-only probe, which is the intuitive statement of the same fact: the message-reading probe separates outcomes better than the board-reading one, or it does not.

The prospective twin is the capped per-instance KL divergence of §8.7, computed from the same two fitted probes but taking no outcome at all. Its agreement with the retrospective score is the test of H3, and it matters because only a quantity computable before the outcome can inform a runtime decision. As set out in §8.7, the twin is non-negative and the retrospective score is signed, so agreement is analysed stratified by retrospective sign and the one-sidedness is reported as a finding about the limits of decision-time measurement rather than as attenuation to be explained away.

### 9.5 Runtime statistics and the gate

A runtime gate cannot threshold CPVI, for the reason given in §8.7, so it thresholds a target-free statistic computable at the handoff and calibrated offline against realised outcomes. Three are evaluated, differing in how much they depend on the fitted probes:

1. **s_info** — the entropy of the conditional probe's predictive distribution over the outcome given the receiver's observation and the message. It uses the offline-trained probe but not the realised label, so it is legitimate at runtime.
2. **s_fail** — a dedicated failure-risk classifier trained to predict eventual episode failure from the same features, emitting a probability of failure at the handoff.
3. **s_cos** — the cosine between the message embedding and a state reference embedding. It depends on no fitted probe at all, and it exists specifically to answer the objection that a probe-based runtime score is circular against a probe-based offline score.

The three are implemented behind one interface in which fitting may see the outcome and scoring never can, which makes the target-free property structural. The Jensen–Shannon divergence between the two probes' predictive distributions is reported in RQ2 as the cheap analytical bridge between the offline and runtime quantities, but it is not one of the three gate statistics; an earlier draft of the abstract listed it as one, and that drift is corrected here.

**Calibration is against realised failure and never against CPVI.** Each statistic is validated on grouped out-of-fold scores against the realised outcome, using AUROC, reliability curves and expected calibration error, with the ECE flagged unreliable below 200 scored handoffs. The operating point is the most aggressive threshold that stays within a pre-registered firing-rate budget of 0.2. Calibrating against CPVI would be circular — it would tune the runtime proxy to agree with the offline measure it is supposed to approximate independently — and the calibration entry point therefore takes no CPVI argument at all, so the error cannot be made by accident.

**The retry prompt must differ from the original, and this is part of the treatment.** When the gate blocks a handoff it re-prompts the sender before the receiver acts. Under greedy decoding a re-prompt with an unchanged prompt is a fixed point: the same prompt yields the same message, the same statistic and the same block, for every bounded retry, so a naive implementation would pass its unit tests and be entirely vacuous in a live run. The retry therefore appends a **versioned feedback template** instructing the sender to state the push direction, whether rotation is required and in which direction, and the goal direction explicitly. Because that template changes what the sender is asked to do, it is part of the intervention rather than an implementation detail, and its version is pre-registered and recorded in the run manifest alongside the prompt version. Raising the decoding temperature on retry was considered as the alternative and rejected, because it would break the determinism story mid-episode and make the gate's effect inseparable from an increase in sampling entropy.

### 9.6 Experiment 1: the usable-information gradient (RQ1)

The first study tests the information gradient and is the dissertation's headline. It runs a factorial design over the five communication conditions, three state serialisations and the difficulty ladder, in self-play with the workhorse model, with seeded start-pose jitter supplying replication. The primary outcomes are episode success, steps to goal and collisions; the boundary outcomes are the mean and the full distribution of per-handoff CPVI, and the PVI − CPVI gap. H1 predicts that success and efficiency fall as the channel is degraded; H2 predicts that CPVI falls with them and explains variance in the outcome beyond the receiver's state, tested by entering episode-mean CPVI as a mediator in a model of outcome on condition with a random intercept for seed and an episode-level variance component.

The serialisation factor is not incidental. It quantifies how much of what looks like spatial reasoning is really prompt formatting — the sensitivity RoCo warns of — and it selects the serialisation used in the rest of the study. A heterogeneous-pair cell tests whether the gradient is an artefact of a single model.

**The absent-versus-unused decomposition.** A pre-registered secondary analysis splits the handoffs of each condition on a two-by-two of CPVI (above or below the within-condition median) against realised progress (positive or negative), and reports two named quantities with intervals: the *absent-signal rate*, the share of handoffs carrying little usable information, and the *unused-signal rate*, the share carrying substantial usable information that the receiver nonetheless failed to act on well. This operationalises the Eccles et al. (2019) distinction between a sender that fails to encode and a receiver that fails to act, on data the main sweep already produces. Its methodological value is that it converts the most likely disappointing outcome of RQ1 — a flat or weak mediation — from a null into a reportable finding about *which half of the channel failed*, which a single correlational number cannot distinguish. The median split is taken within condition rather than pooled, so that the split is not itself a restatement of the condition effect, and the threshold rule is fixed in advance.

This analysis also replaces the learned-message reinforcement-learning comparison that earlier drafts retained as a contrast; §10.4 gives the evidence for that substitution.

### 9.7 Experiment 2: the measurement primitive (RQ2)

The second study establishes the measure as a primitive, on the episodes generated by the first, so it consumes no additional compute. For each handoff it computes the retrospective CPVI, the prospective twin and the three runtime statistics. H3, that retrospective and prospective scores agree, is tested by correlation and a Bland–Altman analysis of their difference, stratified by the sign of the retrospective score for the structural reason given in §8.7. H4, that a cheap statistic tracks the offline score closely enough to gate on, is tested by the rank correlation between each statistic and CPVI and by the AUROC of each statistic for two distinct targets: predicting a low CPVI score, and predicting eventual failure. Both are reported, because a statistic that predicts failure without tracking CPVI would be useful and would also falsify the claim that CPVI is the operative quantity.

The Jensen–Shannon divergence between the two probes' predictive distributions is examined as the analytical bridge between offline and runtime quantities. The encoder-sensitivity check re-estimates the headline quantities under a second pinned encoder; this check is only honest because the embedding cache is keyed on encoder name as well as revision, without which two encoders both defaulting to an unpinned revision would silently be compared with themselves. The conditions under which each decision-time statistic breaks down are reported rather than hidden, since a measure is only useful when its failure modes are known. The outputs the fourth study consumes are the calibrated operating point and the choice of statistic.

### 9.8 Experiment 3a: external validity on real failure logs (RQ3a)

The third study asks whether the controlled-task finding holds outside the simulator. Two decisions define it, and both are departures from the design as first specified.

**The substrate is TraceElephant, with Who&When retained as a comparability anchor.** The conditional construct depends on *s* being the state the receiver actually observed (§8.3), and Who&When (Zhang et al., 2025a) records agent *outputs* only: the system-constructed prompt, the retrieved documents and the orchestrator's framing that each agent received are not in the corpus. Reconstructing *s* by concatenating preceding outputs yields a different quantity from what the receiver saw, and the entire conditional construct rests on *s* being right. TraceElephant (Chen et al., 2026) records `input_context` as well as `output_content` for every step, which maps onto the receiver's observation and the message with no approximation, and its authors quantify what the missing inputs cost: re-running a Who&When failure case to restore the inputs resolves causes that are ambiguous under partial observability, and removing inputs degrades attribution accuracy by a large margin. Who&When is additionally single-class — all 184 instances are failures — so "refit the probes on a held-out portion of the logs" is not merely difficult on that corpus but undefined, since a probe cannot be fitted against a constant label. TraceElephant was adopted partly on the understanding that it supplies both failing and non-failing executions and therefore a genuinely two-class outcome. **That half of the argument was checked against the corpus on 24 August 2026 and does not hold** (E9; `docs/rq3a_schema_mapping.md`): the corpus is 220 traces, every one of them an annotated failure; only its 44 `swe-agent` traces carry an annotation-free outcome at all, and none of those 44 passes. The primary corpus is single-class in exactly the way Who&When is, and the refit arm is undefined on it for the same reason. The observability argument above is unaffected and remains the reason the substrate moved — 5,960 steps and 2,488 inter-agent handoffs with the receiver's input context recorded. What the correction changes is the outcome, not the conditioning state, and it is why counterfactual replay below is load-bearing rather than preferred. Who&When is nonetheless retained for the transfer arm alone, so that the familiar published numbers appear in the same table; every row it emits carries an explicit flag marking its observation as reconstructed, so that approximated and true *s* can never be silently pooled. MAST-Data (Cemri et al., 2025) is used as a cheap secondary corpus for the coarse trace-level question of whether low-CPVI traces carry the inter-agent-misalignment label; it cannot test step localisation, because its annotations are trace-level.

Stated this way the substrate change is not a retreat but a methodological argument in this dissertation's favour: it is the only work in this space that *requires* full observability, because it is the only one conditioning on it.

**The outcome on logs is defined by counterfactual replay, not by annotation.** CPVI needs a per-handoff *Y*, and on a corpus of failure logs the obvious candidates are all flawed. Four were considered:

| Definition | Construction | Circularity status | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Y1 trace success | Did the whole task succeed | Clean — the label comes from the task outcome, not from the attribution | **Unavailable at step level (E9, 24 Aug 2026).** Degenerate on both step-level corpora, which are 100% failures; a constant cannot be predicted. Survives only as MAST's trace-level secondary, where it is confounded with system identity |
| Y2 annotated decisive step | Is this the decisive error step, per the human annotation | **Circular** — trains the probe on the very label the localisation claim is evaluated against | **Forbidden**, and named as such |
| Y3 counterfactual replay | Re-run the system from step *t* with the step's output substituted; *Y* is whether the outcome changes | Clean, and structurally identical to the *definition* of a decisive step without using the annotation | **The definition, not a preference (E9).** With Y1 degenerate and Y2 forbidden it is the only clean route to a two-class step-level target |
| Y4 annotation-free local proxy | Is the receiver's next action schema-valid, or does it advance the recorded plan | Clean if and only if the proxy never references the annotation | Cheap fallback; the proxy design needs its own audit |

Counterfactual replay is the right answer rather than merely a clever one, and the reason is that it makes the epistemology consistent. This dissertation's whole argument rests on the Lowe et al. (2019) position that a correlation between a message statistic and an outcome is not evidence the message mattered, and that only intervention settles it. Defining *Y* by replay applies that same intervention to the *label* rather than to the gate, so the external-validity claim and the causal claim rest on one epistemology instead of two. The technique is precedented at scale: AgenTracer (Zhang et al., 2025b) built a 2,000-trace corpus this way, Causal Agent Replay (Shah, 2026) formalises step-level intervention as a structural causal model with a replay-based estimator, and Jaques et al. (2019) established the same counterfactual logic for measuring social influence in multi-agent reinforcement learning seven years earlier. What is novel here is not replay, which is now well established, but its use to define the *target* of an information-theoretic boundary measure.

The honest costs are two and both are pre-registered. Replay re-runs real, LLM-driven agent systems, so it is non-deterministic: each counterfactual is replayed *n* times, *Y* is the majority outcome, and the replay agreement rate is reported as a data-quality statistic with steps below an agreement floor flagged rather than silently dropped. And replay costs wall time and compute, so it runs on a stratified sample of steps under a hard budget with a dry-run projection produced first. **The pre-registered fallback for a cut replay arm no longer exists in the form written here:** it read "the trace-success label Y1 is computed for every trace regardless, so the refit arm survives even if replay has to be cut", and E9 shows Y1 is a constant on both step-level corpora. If replay is cut, the refit arm goes with it and RQ3a reports the transfer regime alone — which needs no log labels to fit, only `mistake_step` to evaluate, and that annotation exists on all 220 traces. The budget for replay is therefore not a convenience; it is what buys the second regime at all.

**The test and the baselines.** CPVI is computed per handoff under two regimes reported separately and never pooled: *transfer*, applying the simulator-fitted probe directly, and *refit*, fitting probes on a held-out portion of the log corpus with the trace identifier as the grouping key. H5 asks whether low CPVI localises the decisive step better than schema validity and mean embedding cosine, with the published attribution methods tabulated at their reported accuracies and dates. As set out in §2 and §7, the comparison to specialised tracers is made on the operating characteristic — localisation obtained per unit of compute, and whether any of it is available before the outcome exists — and not on raw accuracy, because a logistic probe will not outperform a purpose-trained tracer at localisation and it is not the point of a measure to try. The probe-transfer regime is additionally framed as an explicit experiment with an expected-null branch rather than as a routine second arm: the V-information literature contains no established transfer result, so a null there is a reportable finding about the locality of probe decision boundaries rather than a failure of the study.

### 9.9 Experiment 3b: causal validity of the gate (RQ3b)

The fourth study asks whether the relationship between low usable information and poor outcomes is causal. A subset of the RQ1 conditions is re-run with the gate active: a handoff whose runtime statistic falls below the calibrated operating point is blocked, and the sender is re-prompted with the versioned feedback template of §9.5 before the receiver acts. Success and efficiency are compared against two controls, and gating counts as a causal improvement under H6 only if it beats **both**:

- a **matched-firing-rate control**, which blocks the same number of handoffs chosen at random, isolating the effect of *which* handoffs were blocked from the effect of blocking and re-prompting at all;
- a **random-trigger control**, which fires at random with the same marginal rate, isolating the intervention from the statistic.

The matched-firing-rate control is the one that does the work, because re-prompting is itself a treatment: a sender given a second attempt with explicit feedback may improve regardless of whether the statistic selected a genuinely uninformative message. Firing rate and retry counts are reported alongside the outcome contrasts in every arm. Two limitations are stated in advance rather than discovered at viva. The calibrated threshold is transported into runs whose message distribution the gate itself perturbs downstream, so the operating point is exactly calibrated only for the first blocked handoff of an episode; and a clean null is a legitimate result here and is presented as one, since the design's value is that it can distinguish "low usable information causes failure" from "low usable information accompanies failure", and both answers are informative.

### 9.10 Statistical plan

The primary hypotheses and the analysis are fixed in advance, in a dated pre-registration — `PREREGISTRATION.md` at the repository root, committed before the main sweep — and the analysis protocol maps each hypothesis to a named test and a named artefact.

**Power and scale.** For the headline episode-success contrast at α = 0.05 with Holm correction over four condition contrasts and 80% power, a C0-to-hardest gap of 0.4 requires roughly 25 to 30 episodes per condition, a gap of 0.3 roughly 45 to 50, and a gap of 0.2 roughly 90 to 100. With jitter crossed against seeds supplying true replicates, ten jittered instances by five seeds gives 50 episodes per condition and therefore about **250 episodes across C0 to C4**, on the order of 3,000 to 6,000 macro-steps and 6,000 to 12,000 model calls. The pilot's measured gap selects the row, so the compute request follows from an estimate rather than a guess.

**Inference.** Effect sizes and uncertainty intervals are reported rather than significance alone: Cliff's delta for the ordinal outcome contrasts, bias-corrected and accelerated bootstrap intervals for rates and means. Family-wise error across the condition contrasts is controlled by Holm or Benjamini–Hochberg, stated per family. H2 is tested by an episode-level mediation of success through episode-mean CPVI with a bootstrapped indirect effect, reported both with and without message length as a covariate on the second path (§8.6). Seed sensitivity is tracked on the C0-minus-hardest *gap* rather than on overall success, because overall success varies with everything and the gap is the quantity the hypothesis is about.

**What determinism means here, and what it does not.** Physics and the channel are bit-exact given a seed and are tested as such. Language-model inference under batching is not bit-exact across runs even at temperature zero with a pinned seed and revision, so determinism in this dissertation means low-variance, seed-pinned and revision-pinned, and never exact reproducibility. Every headline result reports seed sensitivity, and the claim of exact reproducibility is not made anywhere.

**Reportable nulls.** Each hypothesis has a pre-agreed reading of its null, so that a negative result is a finding rather than a gap: a flat gradient is a task-design finding about discrimination; a failed mediation routes to the absent-versus-unused decomposition of §9.6; agreement failing only on the negative stratum is the structural limit of §8.7; a statistic that tracks failure but not CPVI bounds the gate's deployability; a null on logs is interpretable only if the replay-based *Y* is defensible first, which is why §9.8 spends its budget there; and a gate null is a legitimate causal answer.

## 10. Execution, validity and scope

### 10.1 Compute, serving and reproducibility

Both agents are served by a single open-weight model behind vLLM's OpenAI-compatible endpoint, one model per GPU job, with the experiment client connecting over localhost on the same node. Decoding is greedy at temperature zero with a fixed seed; the model revision is pinned to a HuggingFace commit hash; structured action output uses guided decoding against a JSON schema. On hybrid-thinking models the reasoning mode is switched off explicitly and the switch is recorded in the manifest, so both roles emit a direct answer and the text scored at the boundary is the text the receiver actually saw. The model ladder is Qwen3 at 8B for the pilot and high-throughput cells, 14B as the workhorse and headline configuration, and 32B as the robustness tier, all Apache 2.0 so that the licence does not constrain reproduction. Compute is a UCL Myriad allocation approved on 23 August 2026: one GPU per job with at least 40 GB of memory for the 14B workhorse in bf16, single node, eight-hour wall time, eight cores and 32 GB of host RAM, with model weights cached on scratch. The projected requirement of roughly 250 episodes and 6,000 to 12,000 model calls is single-digit GPU-hours on a batched 14B, and all analysis is CPU-side and needs no cluster resource.

Reproducibility is enforced through artefacts rather than intention. Every run writes a manifest recording the git commit, the resolved configuration and its content hash, the seed, the exact command, the model revision, the encoder revision, the prompt and gate-feedback template versions, the serving substrate and endpoint, key metrics and artefact paths. A result with an unrecorded revision is not treated as a result. Embeddings are computed once with the pinned encoder and cached by content hash, so probes fit on frozen vectors and no probe fit can perturb its own inputs. Because a pilot may be run on interim compute before the cluster allocation is used, the manifest records the serving substrate explicitly and excludes it from the configuration hash, so interim data is permanently distinguishable from cluster data and can never be silently pooled with it; the standing policy is that interim data informs decisions and headline data is regenerated on the allocated cluster.

### 10.2 Threats to validity, and the branch taken when each fires

Each risk below has a pre-agreed response, so that a risk firing is a decision rather than a crisis. The first is the one that could end the headline.

| # | Threat | What it looks like when it fires | The branch |
|---|---|---|---|
| R1 | Models cannot ground 2D geometry | Self-play success on the unrestricted channel sits below the capability floor, or messages cite coordinates that do not match the true state | One retune only — tighten prompts or change serialisation — then re-gate. If it still fails, RQ3a becomes the headline. The negative is reported honestly as a result about a well-specified task |
| R2 | No measurable gradient | The C0-to-hardest gap in success is negligible, or CPVI does not fall in the right direction | Retune difficulty or serialisation once, then re-gate. The CPVI half of the gate is directional only until the pilot reveals the bit-scale, after which a positive floor is pre-registered |
| R3 | CPVI floor effect | Mean CPVI near zero across all conditions because the receiver's state already predicts the outcome | C3 is the structural guard and is now correctly conditioned (§8.3). The PVI − CPVI gap is reported as a finding in its own right: a large gap is the quantitative statement that most apparent message value was an echo |
| R4 | Probe overfit at pilot N | Training AUROC far above held-out AUROC on 1,536-dimensional features against a few hundred handoffs | The overfit monitor is reported by default; the capacity-reduction rule of §8.5 is pre-registered and applied on pilot data only |
| R5 | "CPVI is just word count" | The first sceptical question, made structural by C1 | The length controls of §8.6 are pre-registered, and C1 becomes the demonstration that construct and confound dissociate |
| R6 | Compute scarcity or queue latency | The allocation lands late, or jobs queue behind the schedule | Default to the 14B workhorse, which fits the whole GPU envelope; the pilot on interim compute converts the eventual window into headline sweeps rather than debugging |

| R9 | Replay non-determinism | The same counterfactual replay yields different outcomes across runs, so *Y* on logs is unstable | Majority vote over *n* replays; the agreement rate is reported as a data-quality statistic and steps below the floor are flagged, with *n* and the floor pre-registered |
| R10 | Online-auditing adjacency | A reader observes that pre-outcome failure detection now exists and asks what is left of the prospective claim | Answered in §7 on three axes — measure versus detector, cost, and validated intervention with matched controls — and reported on the operating characteristic rather than on accuracy |

### 10.3 Ethics, licensing and provenance

The study involves no human participants and no personal data. All models are open-weight under Apache 2.0 and all corpora are used under their stated licences: TraceElephant and MAST-Data under CC-BY-4.0, Who&When under MIT. Counterfactual replay re-executes third-party agent systems against their own released environments and is run under a fixed budget. Trained probes, embedding caches and raw logs are treated as run artefacts and are not committed; only frozen configurations, seeds, manifests, metrics, final figures and a rendered demonstration trace are.

### 10.4 Scope: what is deliberately not done, and why

Two arms present in earlier drafts are cut, and the reasons are evidential rather than schedular, because "we ran out of time" is a weaker limitation than the truth.

**The learned-message MARL comparison is cut because the object does not exist in the suite.** SocialJax (Guo et al., 2025) ships sequential social dilemmas — Coins, Clean Up, Commons Harvest, Territory, Coop Mining, Mushrooms, Gift Refinement, PD Arena — in which agents interact through spatial actions and reward structure, and its shipped algorithms (IPPO, MAPPO, VDN, and reward-shaping variants) are not communication algorithms. There is no message to score, so running the arm would require adding a channel to an environment whose social-dilemma properties are validated without one, porting a communication-capable algorithm into the stack, training it to convergence on the same GPU allocation the main sweep needs, and redefining the measure over learned continuous vectors. The last of these is the decisive objection: the resulting comparison would set frozen natural-language messages scored at inference against learned vector messages trained end to end, confounding the training regime, the message space and the task in a single contrast. The correct statement for the limitations chapter is that the learned-message setting is a *different measurement regime*, not a harder version of this one — IMAC (Wang et al., 2020) and the emergent-communication line optimise a message encoder end to end and presuppose gradient access, whereas this dissertation scores arbitrary frozen natural language at inference with no access to the sender. The absent-versus-unused decomposition of §9.6 delivers the contrast that arm was meant to provide, on data the main sweep already produces, and engages the same literature.

**The principal-agent supervisor arm is deferred, not cut.** Routing the handoff through a supervisor to measure residual agency loss under aligned incentives is the direct empirical test of the assumption in Rauba et al. (2026), and it remains the most valuable single extension. It is deferred behind the four core studies because it introduces a third agent and therefore a second boundary, which requires the unit of measurement to be respecified rather than merely re-run.

### 10.5 Deviations from the design as first specified

This section is a deliberate part of the methodology rather than an appendix, and the case for including it is worth stating. Every deviation below was found by building a cheap diagnostic and running it *before* any result was recorded — an A\* feasibility oracle over the real simulator, a shuffled-message audit, a transcript renderer, and a review pass over the conditioning semantics. Each one, left in place, would have produced a run that looked fine and meant nothing: an unsolvable difficulty read as a coordination failure, a floor-effect guard that was arithmetically inert, a seed axis with no replication in it, a gate that passed its tests and could never fire twice. Reporting them is not an admission of disorder. It is the evidence that the pre-registration and the front-loaded pilot did the job they exist to do, and it is what distinguishes a study whose design was tested from one whose design was merely asserted. Two clock facts bound what the register can contain: zero episodes have been recorded at the time of writing, so no entry below is or can be contingent on an outcome; and the register closes at the F0 freeze, after which any further deviation is logged prospectively in `PREREGISTRATION.md`'s deviation log rather than appended here. The register below is maintained against `docs/experiment_design_log.md`, which carries each entry in dated long form with its trigger, impact and correction path.
